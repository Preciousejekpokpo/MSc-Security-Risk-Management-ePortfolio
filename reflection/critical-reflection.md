# Critical Reflection | Security Risk Management

## How the module changed my perspective

Working through this module has influenced how I think about uncertainty, accountability and the security professional I want to become. I previously associated a strong risk assessment with identifying threats, applying a recognised framework and producing convincing recommendations. I now question what those recommendations establish, whose interests they serve and whether their assumptions could withstand scrutiny. My ambition has shifted towards developing the judgement to advise responsibly when evidence is incomplete, rather than treating technical sophistication as proof of competence.

The development was uneven. Greater analytical capability did not always resolve weaknesses identified earlier, particularly where supporting evidence remained uncertain.

## Learning from outcomes and limitations

In Unit 6, I helped integrate four members' contributions into the Pampered Pets risk identification report. Reconciling differing judgements was challenging because we worked across time zones and relied largely on WhatsApp. Our recommendation for phased digitalisation challenged unsupported expectations about growth, sourcing savings and customer loss, demonstrating an ability to assess proposals against business capacity rather than accept projected benefits uncritically.

The 74% feedback exposed the limits of that contribution. Although the report presented a coherent argument, risk ratings lacked adequate justification and treatments did not show convincingly how residual exposure would change. My role in consolidation made this especially significant. I had resolved differences in presentation without consistently questioning whether the evidence behind them was comparable. Efficient collaboration had produced a completed assessment, but not necessarily a sufficiently challenged one.

This changed how I view collaboration. The aim is not simply to divide responsibilities or reach agreement, but to create conditions in which assumptions can be challenged before they become collective conclusions. My peer evaluation also raised the difficulty of receiving criticism when considerable effort has gone into an idea. I recognise that defending a contribution should involve examining objections seriously, rather than equating criticism with a lack of appreciation for the work.

Unit 11 provided a different test of development. Working independently, I constructed fault trees and a 200,000-iteration Monte Carlo simulation to examine product quality and availability, using sensitivity analysis to identify the influence of containment and fallback. This marked progress from qualitative categorisation towards explaining how failures combine and how results depend on safeguards. However, the 73% feedback challenged the analyst-defined probability ranges, the omission of quantified shared failures and the lack of a reproducible audit trail.

Those criticisms revealed a distinction I had not fully incorporated into my work. Repeating a simulation can make outputs numerically stable without demonstrating that its inputs represent the organisation. Similarly, sensitivity analysis identifies influential assumptions but cannot prove that controls will perform as estimated. My model offered a clearer account of possible risk pathways, not verified predictions of company failure rates. I now regard that distinction as essential to professional credibility, especially where numerical results appear more authoritative than the evidence allows.

Other activities made this lesson broader than Pampered Pets. Threat-modelling literature encouraged me to examine method coverage and operational dependencies rather than assume a named approach was comprehensive. The Unit 5 data protection case highlighted the difference between specifying controls and detecting when they fail, while CVSS inconsistencies made me question the authority of standardised scores. In the Units 1–3 discussion, challenges concerning data exposure and community participation made me consider who can contest evidence and whose interests a decision protects. These experiences reinforced the ethical as well as technical consequences of poorly examined assumptions.

The disaster recovery assessment brought these concerns into a design decision. The scenario required sub-minute recovery objectives, and my proposed multi-region architecture addressed dependencies across checkout, payment and order preservation. Yet without specified capacity, measured failover performance or comparative costs, I could not demonstrate that the solution was feasible or proportionate for a four-person business. The lecturer's criticism of the overlong executive summary added another lesson. Detailed analysis is of limited value if it obscures the decision, while calculations that cannot be reproduced restrict the reader's ability to challenge it. Communicating and assuring technical arguments remain capabilities requiring further work.

## Where I intend to go

My next priority in group work is to make scrutiny part of collaboration from the outset. I would agree scoring criteria and evidence requirements before allocating tasks, use a shared decision log to preserve disagreements and schedule reviews devoted to challenging ratings, not merely combining sections. I would judge improvement by whether contributors can justify final priorities and unresolved assumptions remain visible to those relying on the report.

In quantitative work, I intend to distinguish observed data from published estimates and scenario judgements, document why ranges were selected and test dependencies between failures. Reproducible code or a calculation workbook, including parameters and a random seed, should accompany the analysis. Where sensitivity testing identifies a dominant uncertainty, the next step should be targeted evidence gathering or structured expert judgement, not simply another simulation.

I also want to become more disciplined in translating analysis into decisions. For recovery planning, that means comparing costs and dependencies, testing complete business transactions and stating what evidence is needed before a design can be approved. For executive reporting, it means making the recommendation, uncertainty and conditions for revision visible without burying them in supporting detail.

My development is reflected less in a claim to have mastered risk management than in a more demanding standard for my work. I can now explain complex risk pathways and question the assumptions shaping their outputs. What I have yet to demonstrate is that those methods and recommendations remain reliable when tested against operational evidence. Establishing that assurance, and making my reasoning open to informed challenge, is the professional direction I intend to pursue.
