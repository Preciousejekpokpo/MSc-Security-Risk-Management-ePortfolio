# Critical Reflection | Security Risk Management

*Structured around the What, So What and Now What questions adapted by Rolfe et al. (2001).*

## What

Working through this module has influenced how I think about uncertainty, accountability and the security professional I want to become. I previously associated a strong risk assessment with identifying threats, applying recognised frameworks and producing convincing recommendations. My understanding has since developed beyond the methods themselves towards questioning the evidence behind decisions, the consequences of getting them wrong and the responsibility of making recommendations that others can reasonably trust. This has also shaped my professional ambitions, particularly my interest in work where technical understanding must be balanced with sound judgement, ethical responsibility and the realities organisations face.

My previous experience in healthcare had already made me conscious of confidentiality, safeguarding and the consequences of decisions affecting others. Studying security risk management has broadened that perspective, helping me recognise how similar responsibilities arise in digital systems, business operations and organisational governance. However, the module also exposed a gap between understanding these responsibilities academically and demonstrating that I can meet them in professional practice.

In Unit 6, I consolidated research from four members into the Pampered Pets risk assessment. Working across time zones required asynchronous discussions, largely through WhatsApp. Our report challenged unsupported growth and sourcing projections and recommended phased digitalisation. However, the 74% feedback questioned our risk-score justifications and the absence of convincing residual-risk analysis. I had helped produce a coherent argument without ensuring that the evidence behind its ratings was equally coherent.

By Unit 11, I could develop fault trees, run a 200,000-iteration Monte Carlo simulation and use sensitivity analysis to examine how containment and fallback influenced quality and availability risks. The 73% feedback recognised this technical progression but exposed analyst-defined assumptions, unmodelled shared failures and insufficient evidence that the recovery architecture could meet its objectives.

## So What

What stands out is the difference between completing an assessment and making its conclusions defensible. In Unit 6, I concentrated on integrating contributions and resolving differences in presentation. Yet agreeing on wording did not mean we had resolved disagreement about likelihood or impact. My peer evaluation also showed that criticism could be difficult when I had invested significant effort in an argument. For someone taking responsibility for consolidation, that matters. Challenging my own position belongs within the work, not only at final review.

Unit 11 challenged a different assumption. Numerical models forced me to describe how events and safeguards interacted, an achievement that qualitative scoring alone had not provided. But repeating simulations did not validate their inputs. Sensitivity analysis showed which assumptions had the greatest influence without demonstrating that controls would perform as estimated. I had made uncertainty more visible without obtaining the operational evidence needed to reduce it. That distinction has become central to how I understand professional competence.

Other activities strengthened this judgement from different directions. Threat-modelling literature raised questions about coverage and interdependent systems. The Unit 5 data protection case showed why specifying a safeguard is insufficient when an organisation cannot detect its failure. Reading about CVSS scoring inconsistencies challenged my confidence in numerical labels, while the Units 1–3 discussion made me consider who has the authority to challenge digital evidence and whose interests are overlooked. These connections broadened my view of risk to encompass governance, fairness and accountability.

The recovery proposal exposed the consequences of that wider perspective. Although sub-minute objectives were specified, proposing a multi-region design was not proof that checkout could recover within the target or that the solution was affordable for a four-person business. The lecturer also criticised the length of my executive summary and the lack of a reproducible model. A technically sophisticated recommendation offers limited professional value if decision-makers cannot identify its uncertainties or independently examine the calculations.

The module has not made me ready to approve complex risk decisions independently. I can now explain causal pathways, distinguish scenario estimates from observed rates and recognise where additional assurance is necessary. That is meaningful progress, but it also gives me a clearer understanding of the limits of my experience.

## Now What

I am increasingly interested in cybersecurity roles involving risk assessment, governance and assurance, where technical evidence must be translated into decisions people can act on. To move towards that work, I need experience applying methods to operational constraints rather than academic scenarios alone. I intend to use my portfolio to demonstrate my analytical development while seeking opportunities to practise risk registers, control reviews and business continuity assessments using documented, realistic evidence.

In collaborative work, I will agree scoring criteria and evidence thresholds before tasks are divided, record unresolved disagreements and schedule reviews that question assumptions rather than simply merge contributions. I will evaluate the change by whether another contributor can explain and challenge the final priorities.

For quantitative analysis, I will label observed data, published estimates and analyst judgements separately, test shared failures and retain reproducible calculations with parameter definitions and a random seed. When a result depends heavily on an uncertain input, I will identify the information needed to test it rather than add further modelling complexity.

Over the next six months, I want to develop at least one reproducible risk assessment and a concise, decision-focused briefing, using the feedback from this module as an explicit checklist. I will seek review from someone with relevant technical or risk-management experience and document what changes as a result. This will not substitute for workplace experience, but it will help demonstrate whether my methods and communication are becoming more reliable.

The professional standard I want to develop is not simply producing persuasive analysis. It is being able to explain what a recommendation establishes, what remains uncertain and what evidence would justify acting on it.
