# Unit 5 | Data Protection, Access Control and Organisational Accountability

*Retrospective case study prepared for the final e-portfolio using Case Study 11 from the Irish Data Protection Commissioner's 2017 Annual Report.*

## Critical Review

The Department of Justice and Equality case illustrates how a seemingly minor administrative error can expose weaknesses in organisational data protection. An employee's medical information remained accessible to approximately 80 staff members for up to three years because a document marked private had not been given the appropriate access restrictions. Although the department attributed this to human error, the more significant concern was that its controls neither prevented the disclosure nor detected it promptly, while missing historical access records made it impossible to establish the full extent of exposure (Data Protection Commissioner, 2017).

The Commissioner found breaches of sections 2A(1) and 2B(1) of the Irish Data Protection Acts 1988 and 2003, relating to the unlawful processing and disclosure of sensitive information. The department removed the document and offered an apology, but the employee sought a formal decision instead. As the incident preceded the GDPR, it would be inaccurate to describe the ruling as a GDPR infringement, although the underlying concerns remain relevant to Articles 5, 9 and 32, particularly confidentiality, lawful processing of health information and the effectiveness of security measures.

As an information security manager, I would question why access to sensitive records depended on an individual correctly applying restrictions, rather than requiring authorisation by default. Restricting medical records to designated personnel, reviewing permissions and maintaining retrievable access logs could reduce both unauthorised exposure and uncertainty during investigations, although these measures would still require testing and managerial oversight rather than reliance on technical configuration alone.

This case also sharpens an issue raised in my Pampered Pets feedback, where recommended safeguards were not always accompanied by convincing evidence of their effectiveness. The difficulty is not merely identifying suitable controls, but establishing how an organisation would know when those controls had failed.
