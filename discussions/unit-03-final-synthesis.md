# Unit 3 | Final Discussion Synthesis

## Original Summary Post Submission

I started with the view that data and digital technologies do not automatically create unfairness or accountability problems in human rights investigations. I argued the risks grow when organisations place too much confidence in technological outputs or make design and governance choices without understanding the communities and contexts involved. The discussion helped me see that technical, organisational and social risks are linked rather than separate, and that fairness depends on how these elements interact in practice.

Hancock et al. (2024) show that digital technologies can create challenges for human rights investigations, especially where data, remote tools and investigative practices intersect with questions of fairness, transparency and accountability. My peers extended this by considering the security implications of collecting and centralising sensitive information. This made me reevaluate my initial position. I now see that protecting data is not separate from protecting people, and that security failures can undermine both fairness and accountability. 

The Berkeley Protocol similarly stresses professional and ethical procedures for collecting, analysing and preserving digital information, alongside measures to protect those involved in investigations (OHCHR and UC Berkeley Human Rights Center, 2022).

A second important theme was participation. Costanza-Chock (2020) argues for design approaches that centre communities affected by technological systems, while Pallí-Asperó et al. (2025) show how participatory and stakeholder-driven approaches can be incorporated into human rights research. However, the discussion also highlighted that consultation alone is insufficient if community knowledge does not influence design or investigative decisions. This suggests that participation should be linked to formal decision points, with documented explanations of how feedback affected the final system. This complements the governance focus of the NIST AI RMF, which places risk management across the development and use of AI systems (NIST, 2023).

My main takeaway is that fairness and accountability cannot be treated as properties of the technology alone. They depend on how evidence is collected, secured, interpreted and governed, and on whether affected communities have a meaningful role in those processes. The discussion therefore strengthened my original view that responsible technology in human rights investigations requires technical safeguards to operate alongside human judgement, contextual knowledge and accountable governance. I would now add that organisations should document how community input changed the system, establish independent oversight and provide accessible channels through which affected people can challenge decisions.
