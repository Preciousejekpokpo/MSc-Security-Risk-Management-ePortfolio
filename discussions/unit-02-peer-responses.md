Peer Response 1
Dear Precious

I enjoyed reading your discussion and agree with the points you put forward, particularly your argument that governance failures, rather than technology itself, can be the principal source of risk in digital human rights investigations. I found your distinction between technological capability and the organisational assumptions surrounding its use particularly insightful. It highlights an important issue: even highly sophisticated systems can produce unfair outcomes when their design and implementation fail to reflect the realities of affected communities.

I also agree with your emphasis on overconfidence in technological outputs. Digital evidence can appear objective because it is generated through sophisticated algorithms and data-processing techniques; however, the underlying data, assumptions and models may contain limitations or biases. Recent research on responsible artificial intelligence similarly argues that trustworthy AI requires organisations to address contextual and organisational factors rather than focusing exclusively on technical performance (Hagendorff, 2024). Therefore, human judgement and critical evaluation remain essential when interpreting technology-generated evidence.

Your connection to the NIST AI Risk Management Framework is particularly relevant. Governance, stakeholder engagement and continuous monitoring are essential for managing AI-related risks throughout the system lifecycle (NIST, 2023). I would further suggest that human rights impact assessments could provide a valuable mechanism for operationalising these principles, particularly where digital technologies are used to investigate vulnerable populations.
Your recommendation for meaningful community participation is also persuasive. Participatory approaches can help identify risks that technical developers or investigators may overlook because communities possess contextual knowledge that cannot easily be captured through datasets. Pallí-Asperó et al. (2025), for example, demonstrate the value of participatory approaches in developing AI applications for human rights research, emphasising stakeholder involvement as a means of improving relevance and accountability.

Overall, your discussion provides a strong governance-centred perspective on technological risk. I agree that achieving fairness and accountability requires organisations to move beyond technical bias testing and embed human rights, stakeholder participation, transparency and continuous oversight throughout the technology lifecycle.

Peer Response 2

I strongly agree with your governance-centric perspective. Precious correctly identifies that risk stems primarily from organizational over confidence and flawed design assumptions rather than purely technical limitations. Abinel's suggestion to operationalize the NIST AI Risk management Framework (2023) through human rights impact assessments is an excellent next step. However, to prevent these governance frameworks from becoming mere "bureaucratic tick-box exercises", we must establish structural binding barriers within the investigative pipeline.

To actively prevent governance failures from silencing local knowledge, organizations should mandate the implementation of 'Algorithmic off-switches" or structural dissent protocols. If a data driven system flags a human right violation in a region, but local grassroots networks explicitly dispute the finding based on contextual ground realities, the governance framework must legally or procedurally bar remote analysts from publishing those findings with out an independent , localized reconciliation process (Duffy, 2021).This turns community participation from a passive consultation in to a binding checkpoint.

Furthermore, to address the organizational overconfidence you both highlighted, investigating bodies must enforce probabilistic evidence standardizing. Instead of presenting algorithmic outputs or remote sensing data as definitive proof in reports, governance rules should mandate that all digital evidence be dynamically tagged with a "contextual uncertainty score" derived from local data- scarcity indices (McDermott, 2022). By hardcoding these preventative governance measures directly into standard operating procedures, we can ensure that human rights organizations structurally prioritise human testimony and local expertise over seductive, top-down technological illusions.

My Response



Thank you, Abinel and Anooj, for extending the discussion. I agree that human rights impact assessments and stronger governance procedures could help translate stakeholder engagement from a general principle into something that actually influences how technology is designed and used. This is important because participation can easily become symbolic if communities are consulted but have little influence over the decisions that follow.

As I argued in my initial post, the greater risk is not necessarily the technology itself, but the organisational assumptions surrounding its use. Technical developers may assess whether a system performs accurately against available data, while affected communities may identify language, cultural and contextual issues that are not captured by that data. Pallí-Asperó et al. (2025) support the value of participatory approaches in human rights research, although participation should not automatically be treated as evidence that a system is fair.

I also agree with Anooj that community disagreement needs a formal place within the investigative process. Rather than automatically preventing publication, an independent review could document the disagreement, assess the competing evidence and explain the final decision. Similarly, digital evidence should include information about its provenance, limitations and corroboration, rather than being treated as a definitive conclusion (OHCHR, 2024). This would help reduce overconfidence in technological outputs while keeping human judgement and contextual knowledge central to the investigation.
