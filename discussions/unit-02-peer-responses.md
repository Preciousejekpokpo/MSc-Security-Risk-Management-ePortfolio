# Unit 2 | Peer Discussion and My Response

## Context

Two peers responded to my initial discussion about governance,
digital evidence and human rights investigations.

Their contributions raised questions about human rights impact
assessments, community participation and how disagreements
should be handled within investigative processes.

## My Original Response



Thank you, Abinel and Anooj, for extending the discussion. I agree that human rights impact assessments and stronger governance procedures could help translate stakeholder engagement from a general principle into something that actually influences how technology is designed and used. This is important because participation can easily become symbolic if communities are consulted but have little influence over the decisions that follow.

As I argued in my initial post, the greater risk is not necessarily the technology itself, but the organisational assumptions surrounding its use. Technical developers may assess whether a system performs accurately against available data, while affected communities may identify language, cultural and contextual issues that are not captured by that data. Pallí-Asperó et al. (2025) support the value of participatory approaches in human rights research, although participation should not automatically be treated as evidence that a system is fair.

I also agree with Anooj that community disagreement needs a formal place within the investigative process. Rather than automatically preventing publication, an independent review could document the disagreement, assess the competing evidence and explain the final decision. Similarly, digital evidence should include information about its provenance, limitations and corroboration, rather than being treated as a definitive conclusion (OHCHR, 2024). This would help reduce overconfidence in technological outputs while keeping human judgement and contextual knowledge central to the investigation.
