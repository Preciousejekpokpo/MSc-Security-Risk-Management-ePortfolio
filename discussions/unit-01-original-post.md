Initial Post


I agree with Hancock et al. (2024) that data and digital technologies can undermine fairness and accountability in human rights investigations. However, I argue that technology is not usually the principal source of risk. Instead, the greater threat arises when governance practices create overconfidence in technological outputs while excluding the communities these systems are intended to protect. Hancock et al. (2024) found that many digital tools reflected developers' assumptions rather than investigators' operational realities, limiting their practical value because they failed to address users' needs. This suggests that fairness is compromised less by technological capability than by governance failures that overlook local knowledge and accountability.

This interpretation reflects a broader understanding of risk management, where uncertainty stems not only from technical limitations but also from the assumptions shaping organisational decisions (Aven and Thekdi, 2025). It also aligns with the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, which identifies governance, stakeholder engagement, and continuous evaluation as essential to trustworthy AI (NIST, 2023). In my view, organisations should move beyond technical bias testing and embed human rights considerations from the earliest stages of system design. Meaningful community participation, human rights impact assessments, and transparent accountability mechanisms throughout the system lifecycle would reduce uncertainty, strengthen the legitimacy of digital evidence, and support investigative outcomes that are both more credible and more defensible.
